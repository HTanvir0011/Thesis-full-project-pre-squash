\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Background.} CT can supervise radiograph models, but these may erase lesions, report absent findings or explain decisions poorly,
failures that accuracy metrics miss.
\textbf{Purpose.} To build CT-supervised bone suppressors and Swin radiograph classifiers and test their lesion fidelity, hallucinations
and explanations under pre-specified plans.
\textbf{Methods.} Study~I measures lesion absorption $A$, the fraction of a counterfactual nodule moved into the predicted bone image, and
trains suppressors on public CT-derived projections with a twin lesion-invariance regulariser. Study~II tests whether classifiers gain from
the suppressed images (175 runs, three datasets, capacity-matched controls). Study~III distils a 2.5D Swin CT teacher (CT-RATE) into Swin
X-ray models through paired projections and tests the resulting NIH classifiers for accuracy, heat-map agreement, localisation and
hallucination.
\textbf{Results.} On VinDr-RibCXR the twin ensemble absorbed 0.051 of an added lesion, a released model 0.149 and a matched control 0.178,
at non-inferior bone-region PSNR. Given as separate streams, suppressed inputs did not raise macro-AUROC on any dataset (NIH $+0.0001$
$[-0.0022,0.0024]$); as extra channels they gave small gains (NIH up to $+0.006$). CT
distillation did not improve NIH classification ($-0.0001$ $[-0.0016,0.0014]$), and CT and X-ray Grad-CAM++ maps disagreed. Swin
Grad-CAM++ located expert boxes less than half as often as DenseNet-121's, yet noise flipped far fewer finding-free images for Swin
(27--29\,\% against 78\,\%).
\textbf{Conclusions.} Models of equal fidelity or accuracy differ widely in what they erase, invent and highlight, which should be
audited. A twin regulariser reduces lesion erasure; multi-stream suppressed inputs and CT distillation did not improve classification.
\end{abstract}
